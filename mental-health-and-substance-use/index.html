Mental Health and Substance Use: A Guide to Better Care

By Nazira Usmanova, DNP, PMHNP-BC

Introduction

Mental health and substance use can affect each other in ways that are not always easy to see. A person may use alcohol or drugs to cope with stress, anxiety, sadness, trauma, or other hard feelings. Over time, substance use can also make mental health symptoms worse.

This does not mean that everyone with a mental health condition will develop a substance use problem. It also does not mean that substance use is always caused by a mental health condition. The connection is different for each person.

When both concerns are present, getting help for only one may not be enough. Mental health and substance use care may need to work together so the person can get support for the whole picture.

The good news is that both mental health conditions and substance use disorders can be treated. With the right assessment and care plan, people can take steps toward better health and a more stable life. SAMHSA and NIMH both support care that looks at mental health and substance use together when both are present.

How mental health and substance use can be connected

The connection between mental health and substance use can work in more than one direction.

For some people, anxiety, depression, trauma, or another mental health concern may come first. They may start using alcohol or drugs as a way to feel less anxious, sleep, escape painful thoughts, or handle stress.

For others, ongoing substance use may lead to changes in mood, sleep, thinking, or daily behavior. It may also make an existing mental health condition harder to manage.

There can also be shared factors, such as stress, trauma, family history, and other life experiences. NIMH notes that the relationship between substance use and mental health is complex and can affect people in both directions.

This is why it can be helpful to look beyond one symptom.

For example, someone may seek help for anxiety but also be drinking heavily every night. Another person may seek help for alcohol use but also have untreated depression or trauma. Looking at both concerns can help a provider better understand what the person is going through.

What are co-occurring disorders?

When a person has both a mental health disorder and a substance use disorder, these are often called co-occurring disorders.

You may also hear terms such as: Dual diagnosis; Co-occurring conditions; Mental health and substance use disorders. These terms describe the need to look at both areas of health.

Co-occurring disorders can involve many different combinations. For example, a person may have depression and an alcohol use disorder. Another person may have anxiety and a drug use disorder. Someone else may be dealing with PTSD and substance use.

There is no single combination that fits everyone.

SAMHSA notes that people with co-occurring disorders may have a harder time if only one condition is treated. This is one reason integrated care can be helpful.

Signs that mental health and substance use may need attention

It is not always easy to know when someone needs help. A person may continue to work, attend school, care for family, or handle daily tasks while still struggling. One sign alone does not tell you what condition a person has. A proper evaluation is needed to understand what is happening. If you are concerned about yourself or someone you love, talking with a qualified health care provider is a good place to start.

  • Using alcohol or drugs more often than planned
  • Having trouble cutting back or stopping
  • Using a substance to deal with stress or difficult feelings
  • Feeling more anxious, sad, angry, or withdrawn
  • Having major changes in sleep
  • Losing interest in things that once felt important
  • Problems at work, school, or home
  • Changes in relationships
  • Strong mood changes
  • Repeated attempts to stop using a substance without success
  • Feeling that substance use is taking control of daily life

Why mental health care matters in substance use recovery

Recovery is not only about stopping substance use. For many people, it also means learning how to handle the thoughts, feelings, stress, and life problems that may be connected to substance use. This is why mental health care can play an important role in recovery. A treatment plan may include the following. The right plan depends on the person.

  • Mental health evaluation
  • Therapy
  • Medication when appropriate
  • Support for substance use
  • Relapse prevention skills
  • Family support
  • Help with stress and emotions
  • Follow-up care

Adult psychiatry at ORIGEN Healthcare

For people who need psychiatric support, adult psychiatry care can include evaluation, diagnosis, medication management, and ongoing follow-up. Treating the person, not just the problem

Good mental health treatment starts with understanding the person. Two people can have the same diagnosis but need very different types of support. One person may need regular therapy and medication management. Another may need a higher level of care because symptoms are making daily life very hard. Someone else may need help with both mental health and substance use at the same time. This is why a full evaluation matters. A provider may ask about the following. The goal is not to judge the person. The goal is to understand what support may be helpful. At ORIGEN Healthcare, mental health services include care for conditions such as anxiety, depression, mood disorders, ADHD, and PTSD.

  • Current symptoms
  • Past mental health concerns
  • Substance use
  • Medications
  • Sleep
  • Family history
  • Work and school
  • Relationships
  • Trauma and major life events
  • Previous treatment

When trauma is part of the picture

Trauma can affect how a person thinks, feels, sleeps, and responds to stress.

Some people may try to use alcohol or drugs to avoid painful memories or difficult emotions. That may bring short-term relief but can create more problems over time.

When trauma and substance use occur together, both concerns may need attention.

For someone dealing with trauma symptoms, PTSD treatment may include a full evaluation, medication management when appropriate, and coordination with trauma-focused therapists.

The goal is to help the person understand their symptoms and find healthy ways to manage them.

Choosing the right level of care

Not everyone needs the same level of treatment. Some people can receive care while living at home and continuing their normal daily activities. Others may need more structured support. Common levels of care can include the following. The right level of care should be based on a person's needs, safety, symptoms, and treatment goals.

Outpatient care

Outpatient care allows a person to attend treatment while living at home. This can work well for people who are stable enough to manage daily life while receiving regular support.

What to look for in a treatment program

Finding the right program can feel overwhelming. A few questions can make the process easier. SAMHSA describes integrated care as a way to coordinate mental health and substance use treatment rather than making people move between disconnected systems.

1. Does the program understand both concerns?

If someone is dealing with mental health and substance use, ask whether the program can assess both.

2. Is the care plan personal?

Treatment should not be the same for everyone. Ask how the provider creates a plan around the person's needs and goals.

3. What level of care is available?

Ask whether the provider offers outpatient care, IOP, PHP, residential care, or referrals to other programs when needed.

4. Are qualified professionals involved?

Ask who will provide care and what type of training or license they have.

5. Is there follow-up care?

Recovery does not end after the first few visits. Ask how the program supports people as their needs change.

6. Can the provider work with other professionals?

When mental health and substance use overlap, communication between providers can help create a more connected care plan.

When to ask for professional help

You do not have to wait until a problem becomes severe before asking for help. Consider talking with a qualified health care provider if any of the following apply. A provider can help you understand what may be happening and what type of care may fit your needs. If there is an immediate safety concern, severe medical problem, overdose, or risk of harm, seek emergency help right away.

  • Symptoms are affecting daily life
  • Substance use is becoming harder to control
  • You are using alcohol or drugs to cope with emotions
  • Relationships are being affected
  • Work or school is suffering
  • You have tried to stop using a substance and cannot
  • Anxiety, depression, trauma, or mood changes are getting worse

A more complete approach to mental health and substance use

Mental health and substance use are often connected, but every person's experience is different.

The most useful first step is to look at the whole person instead of focusing on only one symptom. That may mean looking at mental health, substance use, physical health, relationships, stress, trauma, and daily life together.

Treatment can also change over time. Someone may begin with outpatient care and later need more structure. Another person may start with a higher level of care and move to less intensive support as they become more stable.

There is no single path that works for everyone.

What matters is finding care that fits the person's needs and gives them the right support at the right time.

If you or someone you care about is struggling with mental health and substance use, reaching out to a qualified health care provider can be an important first step. You do not have to figure everything out alone.

This article is general information, not medical advice. Talk with your provider about your own health.
